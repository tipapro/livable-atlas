Livable Atlas · How it works

Find places that fit your idea of a good life

Livable Atlas is a free interactive world map for comparing places to live. It brings climate, environment, infrastructure and economic data into one adjustable score.

What does the map measure?

Each land cell on a 0.25° grid, excluding Antarctica, is scored from 0 to 100 using 30 factors in six groups:

  • Climate: summer heat, winter comfort, humidity, sunshine, rain, wind and comfortable months.
  • Country and region: regional GDP per capita, human development, homicide rate and price level.
  • Infrastructure: travel time to cities, proximity to a large metropolitan area and airport, and population density.
  • Natural hazards: cyclones, earthquakes, wildfires and floods.
  • Environment: annual fine-particle air pollution (PM2.5).
  • Geography: distance to water, elevation and terrain ruggedness.

How are livability scores calculated?

The California preset favours sunny, mild weather, comfortable winters, accessible cities and a developed economy. Each factor has a preferred range, a tolerance and a weight. Factor scores are averaged within groups, then combined using group weights. Key factors apply an additional penalty outside their preferred range. No place is excluded for a low score; missing values are skipped.

These are preference-based comparisons, not an objective ranking of the world's best cities. Change the ranges and weights to reflect what matters to you. A group weight of zero removes that group's influence, including its key-factor penalties.

How to use the map

  1. Search for a city or select a place on the map to see its score, factor breakdown and monthly climate.
  2. Adjust the settings and watch the scores update. The Cities tab lists the 20 highest-scoring cities with at least 100,000 residents under your settings.
  3. Pin up to three places to compare their factors side by side. Save your settings in your browser.

Contrast is on by default: colours spread out similar scores to reveal local differences. Read the legend, or turn Contrast off to use the fixed 0–100 colour scale. The numerical scores stay the same.

Where does the data come from?

Sources include TerraClimate, CRU CL 2.0, Natural Earth, ETOPO, GeoNames, GHSL, OurAirports, Nelson travel-time data, ACAG PM2.5, IBTrACS, GEM, GFED, JRC, UNDP, the World Bank and Kummu regional GDP data. Climate datasets include 1991–2020 and 1961–1990 normals; other layers have their own dates. The map's “About the data” dialog lists each source's period, retrieval date, citation and licence.

Read the data provenance and refresh notes, or inspect the source code.

What are the limitations?

A 0.25° cell is roughly 28 km north to south; local neighbourhoods and microclimates can differ substantially. Some indicators are national or regional averages, and missing data does not lower a score. Historical averages do not predict future climate or today's conditions. Visas, housing availability, taxes, personal circumstances and many other aspects of moving are outside the model.

The interface is available in English and Russian. Settings are stored locally in your browser. This is a non-commercial project; the underlying datasets retain their own licences, including GEM's CC BY-NC-SA.
